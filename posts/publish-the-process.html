Publish the Process, Not Just the Product.

October 3, 2026

I used to think building was the hard part.

I was wrong. After spending the last few weeks shipping things — a QR design generator, a YouTube channel, a referral campaign that actually hit its target — I've reached a conclusion that feels obvious in hindsight: marketing is the hardest part.

Not building. Not shipping. Marketing.

Let me explain how I got there, because the conclusion changed what I'm doing.

The QR design generator is done. It's a working MVP, built with Next.js. I'm running it as a "$500 flip challenge" with a target of roughly $17 a month in net profit. Not exactly a moonshot. The deployment to Vercel is still pending because I need to create the API token on my PC, and I keep not sitting down to do it.

That last detail embarrasses me a little. The product works. The thing blocking it isn't technical. It's the unglamorous admin work that doesn't feel like "building." And it made me realize: I can build. What I can't do — or at least, what I've been bad at — is getting anyone to care.

So I made a decision. If marketing is the hardest part, then the honest move is to publish the process, not just the product. Including the failures, the rejections, the mistakes. The stuff that never makes it into the launch post.

This isn't humility aesthetics. It's not "look how authentic I am" as a branding move. It's a strategy, and I'll explain why.

First, the receipts.

On September 27, I opened a YouTube channel. The name is 선우파크, the handle is @sunwoopark0512. The channel description says, in Korean, something like this: "The person who sends the email even without a job posting — documenting attempts across multiple platforms, failures included. Build in Public."

That's the mission statement. Not "I'm a successful creator showing you my system." More like: "I'm going to try things in public and you can watch the ones that flop."

The day after that, on September 28, I published Build-in-Public-themed posts on X and Threads. I didn't wait until I had something impressive to show. I started with the process itself as the content.

Then there's the referral campaign. The code was 8BQXT7, and it hit 30 out of 30. Full. When it hit the target, I deactivated the 18 related crons — the automation I'd set up around it — because the campaign was done. The follow-up campaign, code J7C1I5, was at 5 out of 30 as of September 27. Still running. Still short of the goal.

Notice what I just did there. I gave you a win (30/30) and a not-yet-win (5/30) in the same breath. That's the point. In a normal marketing narrative, you'd only hear about the 30/30. The 5/30 would be quietly forgotten or mentioned later once it succeeded. I'm telling you now, while it's still just five.

Why? Because the process is the product.

Here's my theory, and I hold it loosely: when you're a 43-year-old aspiring solopreneur in Berkeley with no audience and no track record, "look at my amazing results" is a terrible marketing strategy. You know why? Because there are no amazing results yet. If your marketing depends on wins, you have nothing to say for the first two years.

But "watch me try" is a strategy you can start today. Every failure is a data point. Every rejection is content. Every pending token, every half-finished deployment, every campaign stuck at 5 out of 30 — that's all material, and it's honest, and it compounds.

There's a second reason, and it's more practical. Publishing the process forces clarity. When I wrote that the Vercel deployment was pending because I hadn't created the token on my PC, I was annoyed at myself — and the next time I sit at my PC, I'll remember I said it publicly. Even a tiny audience isn't just watching. They're holding me accountable, even if they never comment. It's like working with the door open.

The third reason is the one I believe in most: it filters for the right people. The ones who respond to "I shipped a thing and it got five signups" are not the same people who respond to "I'M CRUSHING IT." The first group includes other builders, other solopreneurs, people who know what five signups actually cost. Those are my people. I'd rather have ten of them than ten thousand spectators waiting for a success story.

None of this means I'm comfortable with it. Posting about a campaign sitting at 5/30 doesn't feel great. There's always the voice that says: wait until it's 30/30, then tell the whole story with the benefit of hindsight. That voice is the marketer in me, and he's wrong, because by then the story is just another victory lap and victory laps are boring.

My operating principle, which I carry over from Korean into English, is this: ship first, get user feedback, fix it, and keep going. "일단 질러 그리고 유저 피드백 받아. 그리고 고쳐서 진행해."

Notice that marketing fits right into that loop. Ship first — that includes shipping the story of the thing, not just the thing. Get user feedback — people respond to the process, and their responses tell you what matters. Fix it — adjust the product and the narrative. Keep going.

So here's my commitment, stated plainly: I'm going to keep publishing the process. The QR flip challenge, win or lose. The YouTube channel, views or no views. The referral campaigns, 30/30 or 5/30. The pending deployments and the admin tasks I keep avoiding.

If the product is ever great, you'll have seen it get there. And if it isn't, you'll at least have seen something real — which, in a feed full of highlight reels, might be the most marketable thing of all. Call it a strategy. It is one — it just happens to be true.
