Your Defaults Are Decisions. Name Them.

October 2, 2026

On September 29, I finalized my daily routine, version 1.

It's a one-week trial: September 30 through October 6. Four fixed anchors hold it together — wake at 08:00, lights down at 21:30, screens off at 22:45, sleep at 23:15. Around those anchors, the smaller choices — and in the document, the ones I never consciously made carry the same label: "내 기본값." My default.

Here's the thing I keep learning as a 43-year-old solopreneur in Berkeley, balancing English classes, tango nights, and gym sessions: "I just did it that way" is not the absence of a decision. It's a decision you're hiding — from yourself first.

Let me explain what I mean, because this one cost me more than any single mistake I can name.

A default is a decision you made once — or inherited and never questioned — and then stopped noticing. You wake at 08:00 because... well, because that's what the routine says. You answer every message the moment it arrives because... that's just what you do. You ask three questions at once because asking feels more efficient than waiting.

Each of these is a choice. The only difference between a choice and a default is whether you can see it.

When I wrote the routine document on September 29, I was forced to look. Every "어차피 이렇게 할 거야" — every "I'll just do it like that anyway" — had to go on the page. And the moment it was on the page, it became arguable. Wake at 08:00? Screens off at 22:45? Those aren't laws of physics. They're my defaults for one week, and on October 6 I get to check them against real data. My rule for the trial: no unnamed changes until then — not because the defaults are right, but because unnamed changes during a trial are just noise. If a default is clearly harmful mid-trial, change it — but name the reason. The point isn't rigidity. It's telling drift apart from damage.

I learned the value of this earlier this month, in a different corner of my life. I run my days with AI assistance, and I made a rule for the setup: whenever it fills in a choice I didn't make — a vendor, a tool, a name, a method — it has to mark it as "내 기본값." My default.

The reason was simple, and it came from pain. When the default was invisible, I couldn't tell which parts of a plan were my judgment and which parts were guesses. So I'd approve a plan and discover, a day later, that a key choice was a coin flip nobody named. Naming it didn't make the guess smarter. It made it reviewable. The most expensive thing in a one-person operation isn't a wrong choice. It's a choice nobody knows was made.

Same story, different shape: I have a standing rule to ask one question at a time, and never move on without an answer. It sounds like a politeness habit, but it started as a cost I finally named. Before it was a rule, I'd fire off two or three questions in a row, and the answers came back shallow — one got answered, the others evaporated. The default back then was "asking more is more efficient." It wasn't. It was decision avoidance dressed up as momentum. Naming it — one question at a time — turned a vague feeling into something I could actually check.

Now, the objection I hear most, including from the voice in my own head: "Isn't naming everything exhausting? Decision fatigue is real."

Yes, it is. But here's the error in that objection: naming a default is not making a new decision. It's admitting the one you already made. The fatigue doesn't come from naming. It comes from re-deciding the same invisible thing fifty times because you never wrote it down. A named default is a decision you make once and review on a schedule. An unnamed one is a decision you make every single day without knowing it.

And: "What if my default is wrong?" Good. Naming it is how you find out. My routine's defaults are wrong somewhere — guaranteed. That's why the trial has an end date and a review. An unnamed wrong default doesn't get corrected; it gets repeated until it costs enough to notice.

So here's the frame I use now. Three steps, in order.

1. Write it down (적기). Find the choice you keep making without noticing. "I always reply instantly." "I always sleep after 2 AM on tango nights." If you do it twice without deciding, it's a default. Start with the ones that cost you when they're wrong — not every micro-habit needs a name. Put the costly ones on paper.

2. Name it (이름 붙이기). Give it a name — even a blunt one. "My default: lights down at 21:30." "My default: one question at a time." A name makes it an object you can point at, instead of weather you live in.

3. Mark it (표기). When a default fills in a blank, say so: "this is my default." And put a review date on it. My routine's review is October 6, after real data. Until then, no unnamed changes. A default without a review date is just a habit with paperwork.

My operating motto, carried from Korean into English, is this: ship first, get user feedback, fix it, and keep going. "일단 질러 그리고 유저 피드백 받아. 그리고 고쳐서 진행해."

Defaults fit right into that loop. Ship the default — write it down and live it for a week. Get feedback — the data comes back. Fix it — change what the data contradicts. Keep going.

The unnamed default is the most expensive one you'll ever pay for, because you pay for it every day and never get the receipt. Name them. All of them. Then let reality argue with you.
