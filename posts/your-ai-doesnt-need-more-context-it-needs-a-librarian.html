Your AI Doesn't Need More Context. It Needs a Librarian.

September 23, 2026

I stopped feeding my AI everything.

That was the first mistake I made as a power user: believing memory was a storage problem. Longer context windows, bigger uploads, every document I owned. If the model could see everything, it would know everything.

It didn't. It saw everything and understood less.

The longer the context got, the vaguer the answers became. Relevant facts drowned in irrelevant ones. My AI had perfect recall and terrible judgment — like someone who reads every book on the shelf and remembers none of them.

The problem wasn't memory. The problem was that I had given my AI a warehouse when it needed a librarian.

Raw context is a haystack

A giant context window is a haystack with a million-dollar promise: the needle is in there somewhere. But attention is a budget. Every irrelevant paragraph you add dilutes the weight of the paragraph that matters.

I watched my assistant cite a plan I'd scrapped three months ago — confidently, because the document was in context and the context was too long for it to weigh what was current. Storage isn't curation. A bigger pile doesn't find the needle. It buries it.

What I needed wasn't more room. It was someone in the room — a librarian who decides what's worth keeping, what's worth finding, and what's worth forgetting.

My three librarian tools

I built my assistant's memory the way a good library works. Three instruments, all maintained by me, all opinionated.

Summary notes. Not transcripts — distillations. After a long conversation, I keep the decisions, the commitments, the why. A page of notes beats fifty pages of chat logs, because the notes already did the thinking. The AI doesn't need to relive the conversation. It needs the conclusions.

Daily logs. One line per day about what happened and what mattered. Days blur for machines the same way they blur for us; a dated line — "Sept 23: decided to kill the A plan, going with B" — gives the assistant a timeline to stand on. Without a log, every day is equally today. With one, the AI knows what changed and when.

Per-person cards. One card per person who matters: who they are, what we've decided together, what I care about in relation to them. When I mention a name, my assistant doesn't search forty thousand messages. It opens a card. A card is a relationship, compressed to what you need to act on.

None of this is automatic. That's the point. Curation is a human verb — and verbs need repeating: a note I don't update becomes a confident lie with my name on it.

Retrieval is curation, not storage

Here's the principle I now run everything on: the quality of an AI's memory is decided at write time, not read time.

Storage asks: how much can we keep? Curation asks: what's worth keeping, and in what form? A summary note written by someone with judgment beats a full transcript retrieved by something without it. The librarian's work happens before the question is ever asked.

This is why the librarian has to be me — or at least start with me. I know what mattered in the conversation. I know which commitment was real and which was thinking out loud. No retrieval algorithm can know that. Judgment is the part that can't be automated, which makes it the part that matters most.

The closing

A bigger shelf doesn't make a library. A librarian does.

Feed your AI less. Curate what you keep. Write the note, log the day, keep the card — and let the retrieval be the easy part, because the hard part was already done by you.

Your AI doesn't need more context. It needs a librarian. Be the librarian.
